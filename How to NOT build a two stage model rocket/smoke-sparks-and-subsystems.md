# Smoke, Sparks & Subsystems

Because building rockets is definitely more than just "light fuse, stand back." This section is our official logbook of what went up, what blew up, what stayed disappointingly close to the ground, and the important (and sometimes ridiculous) lessons we learned with every attempt.

From wet motors to misaligned stages, and from midnight launches to parachute failures — welcome to our rocket opera.

#### Humble Beginnings and Humidity Issues

### More Like a Failed Static Fire

17th August, 2024 (Launch Attempt #1)

The first one. The bold beginning. The start of what would become a recurring series of “learning opportunities.”

We had big dreams, but not-so-big resources. The fins were lovingly sliced out of a hard sheet of cardboard — the type you usually see in art projects, not aerospace applications. The avionics bay? Some wood and hope slapped together. Sure, we dreamed of 3D printed parts, but reality hit: we didn’t have a printer. Or a budget.

The motor was a basic PVC. We thought we’d stored it well, but turns out “well” meant “left it in a humid box and hoped for the best.” Moisture ruined the propellant, and it showed.

After hours of prepping under the sun, we finally hit the launch switch in the evening... and the rocket leapt a whole three meters into the air before flopping back down with a defeated poof.

Naturally, instead of giving up, we did what any tired, slightly delusional engineers would do — flipped the rocket upside down, stuck it back on the pad, and lit the second stage motor just to see what happens. It was a static fire now. We called it Reverse Engineering.

The biggest mistake though? The spray paint. In our heads, it was supposed to look sleek, bold, intimidating. In reality, it looked like a clown with commitment issues. Think orange on blue and read. It haunts us still.

### Woodn't Fly Straight If It Tried

25th October, 2024 (Launch Attempt #2)

Enter: The wooden era.

This one’s special. Why? Because this rocket was, frankly, built like a wobbly table. We had decided to go with wooden fins and a wooden fincan (because why not make it harder?). We thought it would be stronger, sturdier — more professional. And, in theory, it could’ve been... if we had access to proper tools, precise manufacturing, or any actual woodworking skill.

Due to resource “constraints” (translation: no money), we were forced to make do with what we had. The result? A rocket whose two stages didn’t even line up properly. Structurally speaking, it was about as sound as a Jenga tower mid-game.

Structurally, the vehicle was questionable at best. Once launched, it did its best impression of a confused bird in turbulence. The flight wasn’t stable, the trajectory was unpredictable, and the data? Useless. A full-blown aerodynamic tragedy.

But hey — it cleared the pad, so... progress?

### Who Tf Launches at Night?

15th February, 2025 (Launch Attempt #3)

With lessons learned from the previous disasters, we reworked the rocket’s design. Better body proportions, improved fin geometry, and finally — 3D printed components! Finally, yes, 3D printed components. I could cry.  This significantly improved manufacturing accuracy and made the integration process way easier.

But fate wasn’t on our side. We struggled to find a suitable launch location all day. By the time we secured a spot and finished the setup, the sun had set. Like, completely. But we were determined. So we launched anyway.

A nighttime rocket launch might sound cinematic — but trust us, unless you're NASA with tracking systems and spotlights, it's a terrible idea.

The flight? A bit better. The rocket did lift off, but the motor just didn’t have enough oomph. It underperformed again, and the rocket, now a bit heavier due to new parts, struggled to gain altitude. The sims looked great... but real-world thrust-to-weight said “nah.”

The flight was short-lived. But we finally pinpointed the root cause: we needed more thrust.

### We Did It!

25th February, 2025 (Launch Attempt #4)

Redemption arc incoming.

We made no drastic design changes this time — just rebuilt what was damaged from the last flight. The focus was on motor performance. We doped our propellant with IO (Iron Oxide) for improved combustion and thrust. And it worked.

The liftoff was smooth. The first stage flew beautifully. Stage separation was clean. The second stage ignited. We had finally reached the mythical “multi-stage flight.”

But... as always, the rocket gods demanded a sacrifice.

The delay between second-stage ignition and actual thrust was just a bit too long. By the time it kicked in, the rocket had already reached its apogee — and the second stage fired while pointing down. The result: a very enthusiastic nosedive.

To make matters worse, the recovery system almost worked. The parachute ejection mechanism triggered... but the nosecone didn’t pop off because WE DIDN’T ADD ENOUGH PADDING TO PUSH IT OUT PROPERLY. That tiny oversight doomed the whole descent.

Lesson of the day: Always make a checklist. And actually use it.
